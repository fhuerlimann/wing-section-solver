What is solved

The steady, incompressible Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations. Turbulence enters only through an eddy viscosity νt. The default Spalart–Allmaras model computes it from one extra transport equation for ν̃:

∇·(u u) = −∇p/ρ + ∇·[(ν + ν_t) ∇u] ∇·u = 0 ∇·(u ν̃) = c_b1 S̃ ν̃ − c_w1 f_w (ν̃/d)² + (1/σ)[∇·((ν+ν̃)∇ν̃) + c_b2 |∇ν̃|²] ν_t = ν̃ f_v1

The alternative k-ω SST model (Menter 2003) transports the turbulent kinetic energy k and the specific dissipation rate ω. It blends k-ω near the wall with k-ε in the outer flow (blending function F₁), and limits νt where the strain rate is high (a₁ = 0.31). That limiter makes it react more strongly to adverse pressure gradients, so it usually predicts trailing-edge separation earlier than SA. Production is capped at 10 β* k ω to avoid spurious turbulence at the stagnation point, ω is fixed at the near-wall solution 6ν/(β₁y²) in the wall cells, and small ambient source terms keep the free-stream values (Tu = 0.1 %, ω = 5 U/c) from decaying across the domain.

∇·(u k) = min(ν_t S², 10 β* k ω) − β* k ω + ∇·[(ν + σ_k ν_t) ∇k] ∇·(u ω) = γ S² − β ω² + ∇·[(ν + σ_ω ν_t) ∇ω] + 2 (1 − F₁) σ_ω2 ∇k·∇ω / ω ν_t = a₁ k / max(a₁ ω, S F₂)

Where the two models disagree, the result depends on the turbulence model, which is useful to know near stall. In deep stall (beyond about 25° on a cambered section) steady RANS can settle on different solutions depending on the iteration history; use the unsteady mode there.

With SA and SST the boundary layer is fully turbulent from the leading edge; “SST + γ transition” adds a laminar run and its transition (see below). The wall is resolved down to y⁺ ≈ 1 with no wall functions. Freestream ν̃ = 3ν for SA. The far-field boundary sits 40 chords away and includes the point-vortex velocity of the current lift, so circulation does not bias CL.

By default the air is compressible, so the same solver covers road speeds, transonic flow with shocks and supersonic flow (up to Mach 2). Even at 300 km/h (M ≈ 0.25) this matters near stall: on the default case at 15° the suction peak reaches a local Mach number of about 0.87, and CL comes out about 3.5 % lower and CD nearly 30 % higher than in incompressible flow. "Incompressible" is the Mach 0 limit of the same equations.

ρ = (1 + γM²p) / T T = 1 + (γ−1)M² (h₀ − (|u|² − 1)/2) ∇·(ρu) = 0 ∇·(ρu u) = −∇p + ∇·[(μ + ρν_t) ∇u] μ = μ(T) (Sutherland)

Non-dimensional with chord, U∞, ρ∞ and T∞; p is the pressure relative to p∞ in units of ρ∞U∞², h₀ the total enthalpy relative to its freestream value. In a steady flow with an adiabatic wall, h₀ stays at its freestream value (exact for Prandtl number 1, a standard approximation), also across shocks; in URANS it is transported with the ∂p/∂t term. Validation: at α = 2° on NACA 0012, CL rises by 4.7 % at M 0.3 and 16 % at M 0.5 (Prandtl–Glauert 4.8 % and 15.5 %); at M 1.5 on NACA 0006 CL is 0.121 against 0.125 from linear supersonic theory. NACA 0012 at M 0.8, α 1.25°, Re 9 million (SA) gives CL 0.256 and CD 0.026 with an upper-surface shock at about 55 % chord and shock-induced separation behind it.

How it is solved

  1. Sections: NACA 4-digit from the analytic formula, or a built-in section (transonic: RAE 2822, NASA SC(2)-0412, -0612 and -0714, NLR 7301, NACA 64A010, NACA 64A109; laminar flow and low Reynolds number: NASA NLF(1)-0416, Eppler 387) from the published coordinates in the UIUC Airfoil Coordinates Database. The 64A109 is not in the database; it is built from the published 64A210 by halving the mean-line ordinates (design CL 0.2 → 0.1) and scaling the thickness by 0.9, which is how the 6A series is defined (the published 64A410 camber is exactly twice the 64A210's). Each side is fitted with a cubic spline in √x, which stays smooth through the round leading edge. The published trailing-edge gap is removed with a linear thickness ramp and added back by the trailing-edge setting, so “As published” gives the published shape. Behind the concave aft lower surface of the supercritical sections the grid lines converge; a layer where they would cross is rebuilt with more smoothing. RAE 2822 check (Medium mesh, SA, Re 6.5 million, corrected angles): case 6, M 0.725, α 2.31°: CL 0.742, CD 0.0116 (experiment 0.743, 0.0127); case 9, M 0.734, α 2.79°: CL 0.821, CD 0.0192 (experiment 0.803, 0.0168).
  2. Transition (SST + γ): Menter, Smirnov, Liu & Avancha (2015), a one-equation intermittency model on top of SST. γ is transported with production 100 S γ(1−γ) Fonset and destruction 0.06 Ω γ Fturb(50γ−1); onset where the vorticity Reynolds number d²S/ν exceeds 2.2 Reθc, with Reθc = 100 + 1000 exp(−TuL FPG) from the local turbulence intensity TuL = 100√(2k/3)/(ωd) and a pressure-gradient parameter from the wall-normal derivative of the wall-normal velocity. γ multiplies the k production, the k destruction keeps at least 10 %, a limiter term speeds up separation-induced transition, and SST's F1 stays 1 in the laminar layer. Free stream: Tu as chosen, νt/ν = 1, held by SST's sustaining terms; production uses S·Ω (Kato–Launder), so stagnation points do not generate turbulence. The model is bistable: a turbulent boundary layer sustains itself wherever it is. The start-up transient of a run would trip it near the leading edge, so for the first 500 iterations production is held off and a viscosity floor (50 ν) keeps the not-yet-turbulent flow steady; near the wall the floor ramps out over 300 iterations so the boundary layer reaches its laminar thickness, then γ is released. The “Transition x/c” tile and the T marks on the Cf plot show where the boundary layer first carries eddy viscosity (νt/ν > 5). Checks (Medium / Fine mesh, steady): NACA 0012, Re 3 million, α 0°: transition at 0.44 chord on both sides, CD 0.0057 (fully turbulent SST 0.0088); NASA NLF(1)-0416, Re 4 million, Tu 0.15 %, α 0°: transition 0.35–0.36 upper, 0.39–0.42 lower, against 0.41 and 0.52 that NASA imposes for this case in its transition-model verification (Hildebrand et al. 2023), CD 0.0064–0.0062 (fully turbulent 0.0093); at α 5° CD 0.0082–0.0075 (fully turbulent 0.0106). So the model finds the laminar run but places transition about 0.05–0.1 chord too early on these meshes; the published verifications needed several hundred thousand cells to converge transition. Below Tu ≈ 0.2 % the model hardly responds to Tu. Use it for natural-laminar-flow sections, low Reynolds numbers and the drag crisis of a cylinder; for wind-tunnel data taken with trip strips (RAE 2822, most transonic tests) fully turbulent SA or SST is the fair comparison.
  3. Circle: diameter = chord, nodes evenly spaced in angle, plain radial grid lines (no wake steering). Fully turbulent models give a supercritical-like CD ≈ 0.5 at Re 100 000–200 000, where a real cylinder has a laminar separation and CD ≈ 1.2; with the transition model the steady CD at Re 200 000 rises to 0.82. The flow is unsteady, so use URANS for the shedding and its mean drag.
  4. Supersonic sections: double wedge (diamond, shoulder 30–70 % chord), biconvex (two circular arcs) and a wedge with a flat, blunt base, all defined exactly from their geometry; the shoulder corner of the double wedge is placed on a mesh node so it is not cut off. The wedge's base is built like a blunt trailing edge, with up to twice as many base nodes for bases thicker than 2 % chord. Check against linear (Ackeret) theory at M 2, α 2°, 6 % thick, Medium mesh, SA: double wedge CL 0.0814, pressure drag 0.0112 (theory 0.0806, 0.0111; at α 0°: 0.00832 against 0.00831); biconvex pressure drag 0.0141 (theory 0.0139). In supersonic flow the pressure drag of these sections is almost all wave drag; the entropy-based wave-drag estimate is not shown there, since near the section most of the wave energy is still in the pressure field rather than in entropy. A supersonic run first holds Mach 0.05 for 30 iterations, so the start-up overshoot at sharp corners (up to ~30 × U∞ at a base corner) decays before the Mach number is ramped up.
  5. Mesh: an O-grid marched out from the surface along smoothed normals, first cell sized for y⁺ ≈ 1 from a flat-plate skin-friction estimate, geometric growth to 40 chords. By default the trailing edge is blunt, with a few mesh points across its base, so a band of grid lines runs straight downstream into the wake instead of fanning out from a single point. Behind the trailing edge the grid lines of a band of surface nodes (±1/10 of the ring for a sharp trailing edge, ±1/16 plus the base for a blunt one) are steered downstream, so the wake is covered by grid lines running with it instead of a fan from one point: at x/c = 1.5 the cross-stream spacing on the wake centreline is about 0.015 c instead of 0.07 c on the Medium mesh. For a sharp trailing edge this moved NACA 0012 at α 10°, Re 6 million from CL 1.098 to 1.090 (Medium) and halved the Medium-to-Ultrafine difference.
  6. Finite volumes: cell-centred, second-order upwind convection for momentum (deferred correction, bounded), first-order upwind for ν̃, k and ω, central diffusion.
  7. Pressure–velocity coupling: SIMPLEC with Rhie–Chow interpolation; under-relaxation 0.95 for velocity in steady runs with SA and 0.9 with SST (0.85 in URANS, and as an automatic restart if a steady run diverges), 1.0 for pressure, 0.8 for ν̃. The Rhie–Chow smoothing is fixed, so the converged answer does not depend on the relaxation factors.
  8. Linear systems: line-by-line tridiagonal solves, alternating wall-normal and periodic circumferential lines. The pressure-correction equation uses an agglomeration multigrid V-cycle (2 × 2 cells merged per level, down to 12 × 6 cells) with these line solves as the smoother, so pressure errors spanning the whole domain are removed in one cycle instead of creeping outwards one cell per sweep.
  9. Unsteady mode (URANS): dual time stepping. Each physical step adds a second-order backward (BDF2) time derivative to the momentum and ν̃ equations and is converged with inner SIMPLEC iterations until the mass imbalance drops twentyfold. The first half of the run is treated as start-up; means, RMS and shedding frequency come from the second half. By default the run starts from rest (uniform flow, as if the wing were suddenly set moving) and the inflow angle swings +1° and −1° over the first 1.5 c/U. A converged steady solution has nothing left to disturb it, so without this kick a flow that could shed vortices may stay steady. If the run still settles, the URANS solution at that angle really is steady: the turbulence model's eddy viscosity damps the shear-layer instability that sheds vortices, which on this kind of section typically happens only well past stall.
  10. Forces: surface pressure and wall shear integrated over the section. A case counts as converged when the average CL and CD over the last 100 iterations differ from the 100 before by less than the chosen tolerance; Strict means 2×10⁻⁴ (relative) in CL and 2×10⁻⁵ in CD, Standard 2.5× and Quick 5× looser. Reported forces are that 100-iteration average.
  11. Adaptive mesh (optional): after a steady solution, the mesh is rebuilt with the same topology and the solution is interpolated onto it. The indicator is the change of total pressure from cell to cell in each grid direction: it is large in the boundary layer, the shear layers, the wake and vortex cores, but not where the outer flow merely accelerates. Rings are pulled into these regions (up to about 4 times finer), paid for by up to 25 % more rings; behind the trailing edge the grid lines of a ±27° sector are also pulled into the wake. The wall ring and the near-wall rings stay as they are, so y⁺ is unchanged, and the new node positions are smoothed with a stiffness that grows where neighbouring grid lines are close, so cells stay as orthogonal as in the base mesh. The intermediate solutions stop after at most 1,500 iterations; the last one is converged normally. Each new angle of attack, including every point of a polar sweep, is adapted again. On the default case (10°) it lowers CD by about 2 % towards the Ultrafine value and leaves CL unchanged; in stall (20°) CL moves about a third of the way from the Medium to the Fine result. In compressible flow the indicator uses the isentropic total pressure. When the flow is supersonic anywhere, shocks are refined too: a pressure sensor, the second difference of pressure along each grid direction divided by the local pressure, marks them (a captured shock is a jump over a few cells, so the sensor stays large however fine the cells get, while smooth expansions fade). Shock weights go up to 6 times finer and are smoothed mostly along the shock; the whole ring is redistributed, anchored at the leading edge, and grid lines may move from 0.03 chord above the wall (0.4 chord without shocks). RAE 2822 at M 0.734, two passes: the upper-surface shock is about half as thick as on the base mesh; at M 1.42 the bow shock and the trailing-edge shocks sharpen similarly. The wave-drag estimate is capped at the pressure drag, since entropy from the wake can leak into it where trailing-edge shocks border the wake.
  12. Compressible flow: the same SIMPLEC scheme in its all-speed form. Mass fluxes use a bounded upwind-biased face density; the pressure correction also corrects the density (∂ρ/∂p = γM²/T), which adds a convective term to its equation: it vanishes at low speed and carries corrections downstream in supersonic regions, which is what lets shocks form. The far field follows the characteristics: subsonic inflow fixes velocity and temperature, subsonic outflow fixes the pressure, supersonic inflow fixes everything and supersonic outflow nothing; the imposed state includes the point vortex in its Prandtl–Glauert form. Each run from uniform flow ramps the Mach number up over the first 300 iterations, and above Mach 0.6 the velocity relaxation is 0.7. Wave drag is estimated from the entropy the shocks produce (Oswatitsch), integrated over the shock region found with the normal Mach number u·∇p / (a|∇p|) > 1 outside the boundary layer; it is part of the pressure drag and only a rough estimate, since a captured shock smears the entropy over several cells. Simplifications: the viscous stress omits the small dilatation terms, and with strong shocks and shock-induced separation a steady run may keep oscillating without converging. Then use URANS: it settles where the flow is steady (NACA 0012 at M 0.75, α 6°: CL 0.473), and shows the shock oscillation where the flow buffets.
  13. Velocity vectors: arrows on a regular grid of the view (every 14 pixels), with the velocity interpolated from the mesh like the streamlines. Their length grows with the square root of the speed, so the slow flow in vortex cores and recirculation zones, which no streamline from the inflow reaches, still shows its direction.
  14. Smoke probe: holding the mouse button releases tracer particles at the pointer, carried with the computed velocity (bilinear interpolation, midpoint steps) at the physical rate of the case, or in slow motion. Joining them in release order draws the smoke filament. At real time and road speed the air crosses the view within a frame, so the filament is shown directly along the streamline through the probe, as a real smoke wand looks to the eye. In a steady solution the filament lies on that streamline; during a URANS run it follows the changing field. Shift-drag still pans the view.
  15. Pulses: every 0.2 view widths of free-stream travel, a rake at the left edge of the view releases a row of tracer particles. Each is carried with the interpolated velocity (midpoint steps of a few pixels) and drawn as its own path over the last 0.07 view widths of travel, so the streak length shows the local speed and its curvature the path. In a URANS movie the particles move on movie time through the changing field, so they collect in the shear layers and are caught and carried by the shed vortices; they restart when the movie loops or is scrubbed. In a steady solution they run at a fixed visual rate, with the free stream crossing the view in about 4 seconds.
  16. URANS movie: with "Record movie" ticked, the solver stores a compact snapshot (velocity, pressure, eddy viscosity, wall Cp and Cf) every few time steps. The player blends linearly between snapshots, so playback is smooth at any slow-motion setting, and the field, the Cp and Cf plots, the separation tile and the smoke probe all follow the movie time. Colour scales are frozen on the final field while the player is open. "Save video" renders every frame at its exact movie time and encodes it with WebCodecs into MP4 with H.264 (WebM with VP9 in browsers without an H.264 encoder; 30 frames per second, with the case, time and forces burned in), so the video speed does not depend on how fast the computer draws. The smoke probe keeps releasing smoke at its last position during the export.

It runs in a background thread of your browser. Expect 10–40 seconds per case on the medium mesh, and several minutes on Ultrafine. Beyond about 18° the flow is largely separated, and a steady solver may only reach the iteration limit. Section results are 2D: real wings add tip vortices and induced drag, and F1 wings run in ground effect, which this solver does not model.
